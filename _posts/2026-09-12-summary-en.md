## [Mathematicians decry OpenAI&\#x27;s methods as &\#x27;severe misalignment&\#x27; in AI math](https://mathandai.org/) ⭐️ 9.0/10

On September 11, 2026, Terence Tao published a blog post titled &\#x27;A severe misalignment of AI in mathematics,&\#x27; and The Economist reported that leading mathematicians are outraged by OpenAI&\#x27;s methods, amid a contested claim that OpenAI agents solved a Millennium Prize problem. The controversy centers on how AI companies use mathematics as a benchmark and how credit should be assigned. The dispute is not just about priority or credit; it raises broader questions about whether AI-generated proofs can advance mathematical understanding and whether commercial incentives are distorting academic research culture. It affects mathematicians, AI labs, journals, funders, and anyone relying on mathematical results as trustworthy knowledge. Tao&\#x27;s post labels the situation a &\#x27;severe misalignment,&\#x27; while The Economist notes critics argue AI companies solve math problems mainly to benchmark model strength, which they say is detrimental to mathematics. The specific flashpoint appears to be a claimed OpenAI solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem, with disputes over whether it was independent from prior work by Buckmaster and Alpöge.

hackernews · meredydd · Sep 11, 17:45 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49662371)

**Background**: Terence Tao is a Fields Medal-winning mathematician at UCLA known for work across partial differential equations, combinatorics, harmonic analysis, and number theory. &\#x27;AI alignment&\#x27; normally refers to making an AI system&\#x27;s goals and behavior match human values and intentions; Tao&\#x27;s title repurposes the term to describe commercial AI incentives clashing with mathematical norms. The Millennium Prize Problems are seven famously hard mathematical questions, including Navier–Stokes, each carrying a $1 million prize.

## [Developer: 60% of $220 Google Ads app installs were bots](https://dayzlegame.com/blog/google-ads-bot-farm/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A developer spent $220 on Google Ads to promote their app and, after analyzing the resulting traffic, concluded that roughly 60% of the installs came from bot networks. The write-up on dayzlegame.com triggered a 265-point, 150-comment Hacker News discussion about ad-platform fraud and how to filter it. Ad fraud inflates cost-per-install and corrupts the learning data of Google&\#x27;s automated app campaigns, so indie developers and small advertisers can burn real budgets on non-human traffic. It also raises platform-accountability questions, since the same Google that delivered the bots can ban developers via AdMob for invalid traffic. Commenters note that bot networks usually run from data centers rather than residential ISPs, so advertisers can block whole network ranges via Google Ads &gt; Admin &gt; Account Settings &gt; IP Exclusions, and one advertiser says their US exclusion list already exceeds 4,000 networks. Detection remains hard because click farms use real devices and residential proxies, and industry reports show fraud rates that can still exceed 60% even after platform filters run.

hackernews · nickabe · Sep 11, 18:24 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49662990)

**Background**: Google App Campaigns \(formerly Universal App Campaigns\) are Google&\#x27;s automated ad format that spreads spend across Search, the Play Store, YouTube and the Display network to drive app installs, typically billed on a cost-per-install basis. Mobile ad fraud is generated by bots, click farms, device emulators and residential proxy networks that manufacture fake clicks and installs; residential proxies are especially effective because the traffic appears to come from real consumer connections. AdMob is Google&\#x27;s mobile ad monetization network, and it routinely bans developers or withholds payments when their apps are found to generate invalid traffic.

## [EPA Proposes Scrapping Public Review for Data Center Pollution Permits](https://capitalbnews.org/data-centers-permit-rules-epa/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The EPA has proposed a rule that would scrap the federal requirement that states notify the public and hold a public comment period before approving air pollution permits for industrial facilities, including the data centers rising across the country. The agency held a public hearing on the proposal on Wednesday, and the change would hand states broad discretion over how — or even whether — the public participates in the permitting process. Data centers are a fast-growing source of air pollution because of the diesel generators and gas turbines used for primary and backup power, so removing public notice and comment would make it much harder for residents to learn about or challenge new facilities. The move lands as AI and cloud infrastructure buildouts collide with local opposition and grid constraints, and it fits the EPA&\#x27;s broader deregulatory push. Advocates warn that without public review, facilities such as data centers may be able to avoid major-source pollution controls altogether, and permits could effectively be issued behind closed doors. The proposal concerns how states handle public participation for certain new sources of air pollution, and earlier EPA guidance already allowed states to run comment periods concurrently with the agency&\#x27;s review of Title V permits.

hackernews · doener · Sep 11, 18:05 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49662672)

**Background**: Under the Clean Air Act&\#x27;s New Source Review program, permitting authorities normally must publish a notice, allow a public comment period \(usually 30 days\), and offer a public hearing before approving air pollution permits for new industrial sources. Data centers rely on stationary combustion turbines and engines for primary and backup power, and those units are subject to new source performance standards \(NSPS\) and hazardous air pollutant standards \(NESHAP\). The current EPA has already issued guidance to streamline Title V operating permit processing and created a Clean Air Act resource page for data center developers, part of a wider effort to speed up permitting for AI infrastructure.
