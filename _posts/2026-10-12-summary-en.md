## [Hacker News Debates the Widening Divide in Perceptions of LLM Capability](https://twitter.com/karpathy/status/2109361546505966046) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A Hacker News discussion thread sparked by an Andrej Karpathy post about the widening gap in shared understanding of LLM capability drew 257 points and 416 comments, with participants sharply disagreeing over how transformative large language models actually are. The thread became a meta-debate about who gets to define &quot;capability&quot; — AI researchers and heavy users who report dramatic productivity gains versus skeptical developers and an unconvinced general public. The thread captures a real fracture in the AI/ML community: the same technology is described as a world-changing breakthrough by some practitioners and as overhyped noise by others, which complicates hiring, tooling investment, and how engineering teams justify AI adoption. It also highlights a trust problem — if experts cannot agree on what these models can reliably do, the public&\#x27;s expectations and policy debates around AI will remain poorly grounded. Commenters framed the divide as between those who insist you still need to understand a codebase for maintenance, token-cost control, optimization and avoiding &quot;code erosion,&quot; and those who never review generated code and simply trust the agent&\#x27;s output. Others pushed back that demand for these models is largely hypothetical, asking what the general public is actually supposed to \*do\* with agentic LLMs when it has no concrete use for them.

**Background**: Andrej Karpathy is a well-known AI researcher and educator, a founding member of OpenAI and former director of AI at Tesla, whose posts on X frequently frame debates about how capable and how useful large language models really are. Hacker News is Y Combinator&\#x27;s long-running tech forum, where submissions are ranked by points and discussion depth, so a thread with hundreds of comments typically signals genuine controversy rather than consensus. The term &quot;agentic LLM&quot; refers to models that can autonomously execute multi-step tasks — such as editing a codebase — rather than merely answering a single prompt. The dispute in the thread echoes a broader tension between benchmark-measured model capability and the perceived, real-world usefulness that ordinary people experience.

**Discussion**: Sentiment was largely skeptical rather than celebratory. One commenter listed ways AI has failed to improve average people&\#x27;s lives — unchanged cars, dishwashers and commutes, higher fuel, food, housing and electronics costs — while another argued the real split is between engineers who understand long-term code maintenance costs and those who never review AI output. A third asked what the general public is even supposed to do with agentic models, and one commenter offered a sardonic timeline \(2015 taxis, 2020 currency, 2021 delivery, 2022 NFTs, 2024 programming\) implying each &quot;world-transforming&quot; tech promise has faded.

Terence Tao published the slide deck for his Caltech talk &quot;Math 2.0,&quot; which lays out the historical &quot;Math 1.0&quot; state of mathematics and the present AI-induced transformation of the field; according to his blog, the talk was postponed to October and substantially rewritten in light of recent events. The deck quickly became a focal point of discussion, drawing a 323-point Hacker News thread with 374 comments. Tao is one of the most prominent mathematicians alive, so his framing of AI as a structural disruption to mathematical practice carries unusual weight in shaping how the field, funders, and institutions respond. The talk signals that AI-driven discovery is already affecting everyday research life — from grant proposals to the choice of open problems — rather than being a distant theoretical prospect. The material is a slide deck rather than a peer-reviewed paper, and Tao notes that his own field, &quot;largely stable for almost a century,&quot; has proven particularly susceptible to this type of disruption. Slides 12 and 13 reportedly address the psychological and sociological fallout, including researchers who had to rewrite just-submitted grant requests after AI produced solutions to problems they had worked on for years.

## [Passkeys Are a Trap: Hacker News Debates the WebAuthn Tradeoffs](https://nonfunctional.substack.com/p/passkeys-are-a-trap-do-not-use-them) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A critical Substack essay titled &quot;Passkeys are a trap&quot; argued that passkeys carry hidden downsides, sparking a 282-point Hacker News thread with 159 comments on attestation, credential portability and device lock-in. The author frames passkeys as a trap for users who want control over their credentials, while commentators pushed back on several of the article&\#x27;s claims. Passkeys are being rolled out by Apple, Google, Microsoft and major services as the default passwordless login method, so questions about portability, device lock-in and vendor control affect hundreds of millions of users. If the criticisms hold, adoption could stall or fragment; if they don&\#x27;t, the debate helps clarify what the standard actually guarantees. Commenters noted that attestation can only be required at credential registration, not retroactively at login, and that browsers prompt the user for permission when a site requests attestation — so broad attestation-based lock-in is unlikely. Others emphasized that passkeys are domain-bound, which makes them resistant to man-in-the-middle phishing attacks, a security benefit the article mentions only in a final addendum footnote.

hackernews · laurex · Oct 11, 21:59 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50048312)

**Background**: Passkeys are built on the FIDO2 standard: WebAuthn is the browser API, CTAP is the protocol to the authenticator, and a passkey is the credential type, all using public-key cryptography where the private key stays on the user&\#x27;s device and only the public key is shared with the service. Attestation is an optional WebAuthn feature in which the authenticator proves what hardware or software created the credential, and it is disabled by default. Because private keys are typically synced through iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager or similar platform vaults, critics worry users lose control over backup, portability and credential strength.

## [NYC&\#x27;s &quot;Click to Cancel&quot; Subscription Rule Takes Effect](https://www.nyc.gov/main/click-to-cancel) ⭐️ 7.0/10

New York City&\#x27;s &quot;Click to Cancel&quot; rule is now in effect, requiring businesses that sell subscriptions or recurring charges to NYC consumers to let them cancel through a simple, readily accessible mechanism rather than forcing them through retention calls or buried account pages. The rule effectively codifies the principle that cancelling should be as easy as signing up. The rule directly affects how subscription-based software, media, and services are designed for a major market, forcing companies to rethink retention dark patterns and cancellation flows that have long been a source of consumer complaints. It also adds momentum to a broader regulatory wave, with California already enforcing a similar law and the EU expected to follow with continent-wide rules. Enforcement is expected to fall to the city&\#x27;s consumer protection agency, which can pursue civil penalties against non-compliant businesses, but as commenters note, the practical test will be how actively the city enforces once public attention fades. The rule targets automatic renewals and hard-to-find cancellation paths, yet it does not obviously prevent workarounds such as cancellation fees or minimum-term commitments.

hackernews · gregsadetsky · Oct 11, 18:01 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50045946)

**Background**: Subscription &quot;negative option&quot; billing — where a service auto-renews unless the customer actively cancels — has become a standard business model for streaming, SaaS, newspapers, and fitness apps. Regulators argue that many companies make cancelling deliberately difficult, for example by requiring a phone call or a chat with a retention agent, even when signup took one click. The &quot;click to cancel&quot; concept requires that the cancellation method be at least as simple as the signup method; California enacted such a law in 2023, and the US Federal Trade Commission has pursued a similar national rule that has faced legal challenges. The EU&\#x27;s forthcoming Digital Fairness Act is expected to include comparable provisions.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely welcomed the rule but focused on enforcement: one long-time NYC resident said the city legislates eagerly yet enforces inconsistently, while another noted Adobe technically offers click-to-cancel but charges an early-termination fee equal to the remaining months of a one-year commitment. Others pointed to California&\#x27;s already-working version of the law and to the EU&\#x27;s Digital Fairness Act, expected to be tabled by the European Commission in November with the same click-to-cancel principle.
