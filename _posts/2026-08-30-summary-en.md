## [DHS uses obscure 1509 summons to snoop on journalists and nonprofits](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/29/trump-dhs-1509-summons-records-journalists-nonprofits) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security has used an obscure administrative tool, the 1509 summons, to secretly obtain records of journalists, nonprofits, and unions without judicial oversight. In one case, DHS obtained six months of T-Mobile phone records for a journalist, while Google declined to comply. This matters because the 1509 summons allows the government to bypass the Fourth Amendment&\#x27;s usual warrant requirement and secrecy rules, affecting journalists, activists, and ordinary people whose data sits with third parties. It raises serious civil liberties and privacy concerns, especially as the tool appears to be used beyond its intended customs-related purpose. A 1509 summons is an administrative subpoena issued under 19 U.S.C. § 1509 for examining records in customs enforcement, with no judge involved in issuing it. DHS has repeatedly withdrawn summonses once challenged in court, which critics say is a strategy to avoid a binding ruling on its legality.

**Background**: The 1509 summons comes from 19 U.S. Code § 1509, which gives customs officials the power to examine books and witnesses and to issue summonses for records in customs-related investigations. Because it is an administrative subpoena, it does not require a warrant based on probable cause or prior approval from a judge. A 2017 DHS Office of Inspector General report already cited CBP for misusing this summons power, and civil liberties groups say ICE and CBP have increasingly used it to demand data from tech companies and telecoms in leak investigations and other non-customs matters.

## [EU AI Act Moves into Real Enforcement Phase: New Rules Already in Force](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiogFBVV95cUxNamVsRjdzOFlRQURhYW9OS09aWC1HYnhaeUFmSmx0anZxOTdkY2RHeEJQc0dQMHJLTDRDMkVxbllPa0Z3UEhtei01aWlsTVFOM2NvLWxIaXhMc3FZNGU0d0lFWW91WEM5MC12Tk9UZHVNLUkxNmNCOFV3RDMtWVZ0NWdIQTR3TXBQbmxYeWlhc0lZUFRIbkl1ZDRnQXVDTUVkU3c?oc=5) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The EU AI Act has moved into its real enforcement phase, with the first binding rules now in force. The regulation entered into force on 1 August 2024, and its provisions are being phased in over 6–36 months, with the initial prohibitions on unacceptable-risk AI practices becoming applicable around February 2025. As the world&\#x27;s first comprehensive AI law, the EU AI Act sets a global regulatory precedent and will affect any organization that provides or deploys AI within the EU, including providers outside the bloc serving EU users. Companies must now align with the risk-based obligations, such as bans on certain practices and transparency requirements. The AI Act classifies AI systems into risk levels — unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal — plus a separate general-purpose AI category. High-risk systems must undergo conformity assessments and meet security, transparency, and quality obligations, while limited-risk systems only have transparency obligations, and minimal-risk systems are unregulated.

**Background**: The EU AI Act is a European Union regulation that establishes a common legal and regulatory framework for artificial intelligence. It was proposed by the European Commission in April 2021, approved by the European Parliament in March 2024 and unanimously by the EU Council in May 2024, and entered into force on 1 August 2024. The Act is a form of product regulation: it places duties on AI providers and professional users rather than creating individual rights. It also applies extraterritorially, similar to the GDPR, and creates a European Artificial Intelligence Board for cooperation and compliance.
